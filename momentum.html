Momentum: why heavy, fast things are hard to stop

First think about it, then test your thinking with the simulator below.

Start with what you know

A bicycle and a loaded lorry both roll toward you at the same speed. Which is harder to stop, and why? Now keep the lorry the same but let the bicycle go twice as fast. What changes?

Both the mass and the velocity matter. Physicists combine them into one quantity:

p = m v

Momentum p is measured in kg m/s. It has direction, so we give it a sign: right is positive and left is negative. When objects collide and no outside force acts, the total momentum before equals the total momentum after.

Investigate

  1. Set equal masses, an elastic collision, and block 2 at rest. Describe what happens to each block.
  2. Make block 1 much heavier than block 2. Then reverse the masses. What does the lighter block do each time?
  3. Choose an inelastic collision. Is total momentum conserved? Is kinetic energy? Where could the missing energy have gone?
  4. Give block 2 a negative velocity so the blocks meet head-on. Predict the result first, then check.

Check your thinking

Investigation 1

The blocks swap velocities: block 1 stops and block 2 moves off with block 1’s original velocity.

Investigation 2

A heavy block barely slows and sends the light block off faster than itself. A light block bounces back from a heavy block that is hardly moved.

Investigation 3

Total momentum is conserved. Kinetic energy is not: some becomes heat, sound and deformation of the blocks.

Investigation 4

Use signs. Total momentum is m₁v₁ + m₂v₂, with left negative. Whatever that total is before, it is the same after.
